Chapter 01

Why the World MovesThe Story and Scale of Travel

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Explain what travel and tourism mean, and how a tourist is different from a regular traveller.
  • Trace the history of tourism from pilgrimages and trade routes to budget airlines and booking apps.
  • Describe the economic impact of tourism on India and the world, using verified figures.
  • Identify the main sectors of the industry and how they depend on one another.
  • Classify the major types of tourism and match them to real Indian examples.
  • Name the key global and Indian organisations, such as UN Tourism, WTTC and IATA, and say what each one does.

What Is Travel and Tourism?

Travel simply means moving from one place to another. Your daily bus ride to the market is travel. Tourism is a special kind of travel: people go to a place outside their usual surroundings for leisure, business, pilgrimage, health or other purposes, and they stay there for less than a year. UN Tourism, the United Nations agency for tourism, uses this idea as its working definition. So a family from Secunderabad spending four nights in Munnar are tourists. A man who commutes from Medchal to Hitech City every day is only a traveller, however tired he looks.

There are two main types of visitors. A tourist stays at least one night at the destination. An excursionist or same-day visitor comes and goes on the same day, like a group from Hyderabad that drives to Ramoji Film City in the morning and is home by dinner. This difference matters for businesses. A tourist spends on rooms, dinners and souvenirs; an excursionist spends mostly on entry tickets, snacks and fuel.

Tourism is also described by direction. Domestic tourism is when residents travel inside their own country, such as a Telangana family visiting Goa. Inbound tourism is when foreigners visit India. Outbound tourism is when Indians go abroad, say to Dubai or Bali. A small Indian tour operator usually begins with domestic tourism because it needs no visas, no foreign currency and far less paperwork.

The Tourism System

Think of tourism as a chain with three links. The generating region is where tourists live (Hyderabad). The transit route is how they get there (a train, flight or a tempo traveller on NH-44). The destination region is where they spend their time (Valparai or Kerala). A tour operator's job is to make all three links feel smooth, so the customer remembers the tea gardens and not the confusion at Coimbatore station.

On the road, off the record

Every tourist has two suitcases: one for clothes, and an invisible one full of "But the photo on the website looked bigger."

A Short History of Tourism

People travelled long before the word "tourism" existed. In ancient India, pilgrims walked to Kashi, Rameswaram and Puri, and dharamshalas along the way gave them free or cheap shelter. Traders on the Silk Route and the spice routes of the Malabar Coast carried goods, ideas and stories across continents. Chinese travellers such as Faxian and Xuanzang came to India to study Buddhism and wrote detailed accounts of what they saw. In that sense, they were among the first travel bloggers, just without likes.

From Grand Tours to package tours

In Europe, between the 17th and 19th centuries, young rich men went on a Grand Tour of France and Italy to "finish" their education. The real turning point came in 1841, when an Englishman named Thomas Cook organised a railway trip for about 500 people from Leicester to Loughborough for a temperance meeting. He bundled the train ticket and food into one price. That was the birth of the package tour, the same idea Travel Kahani sells today. The company Thomas Cook later came to India in the 19th century, and Thomas Cook (India) still operates here as a separate company.

The modern era

The 20th century brought three big changes: paid holidays for workers, the motor car, and the jet aeroplane. Air travel became cheaper after the 1970s, and low-cost airlines like IndiGo (started 2006) made flying normal for the Indian middle class. In India, the launch of IRCTC's online ticketing in the early 2000s, followed by sites like MakeMyTrip and apps like ixigo, moved booking from the travel agent's counter to the customer's phone.

When you tell customers a destination's history, keep it to three interesting facts. Nobody on a tempo traveller wants a lecture, but everybody loves one good story.

The Economic Impact of Tourism

Tourism is one of the largest industries in the world. According to the World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC), travel and tourism contributed USD 11.6 trillion to global GDP in 2025, about 9.8% of the world economy, and supported around 366 million jobs worldwide. UN Tourism estimates that about 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals were recorded in 2025, a new record after the pandemic. These numbers are revised every year, so always quote the latest report.

For India, WTTC's 2026 research says travel and tourism generated about USD 263.6 billion in 2025, roughly 6.6% of India's GDP, and supported about 46.2 million jobs. The most important point for a small operator is this: domestic visitors made up about 86% of all tourism spending in India in 2025. In other words, Indians travelling inside India are the backbone of the industry. Official Ministry of Tourism data also shows India recorded about 2,948 million domestic tourist visits in 2024 (one person visiting three states counts as three visits).

Direct, indirect and induced impact

Economists split tourism's impact into three layers. The direct impact is money spent on hotels, transport, guides and restaurants. The indirect impact is the spending those businesses make in turn, such as a hotel buying vegetables from local farmers or a cab owner buying tyres. The induced impact is what employees spend from their salaries. This chain of spending is called the multiplier effect: one tourist's rupee travels through many hands.

Worked example

A Hyderabad family of four books a 3-night Araku Valley package for ₹36,000. Roughly ₹15,000 might go to the resort, ₹9,000 to the vehicle owner and driver, ₹3,000 to food stalls and restaurants, ₹2,000 to coffee and tribal handicraft sellers, ₹1,500 to entry tickets and the Borra Caves, and the operator keeps the balance as margin and costs. The resort then pays its cooks and housekeeping staff, who spend at local shops. One booking has quietly fed a dozen households.

Costs of Tourism

Tourism is not all sunshine. Too many visitors can cause overtourism: crowded hill stations, traffic jams in Manali, plastic waste on trekking trails and rising rents for local people. Seasonal jobs can disappear after the peak months. A responsible operator plans around these problems, for example by choosing smaller homestays and off-peak dates.

Case study

Kerala's Responsible Tourism Mission (real): Kerala began a responsible tourism programme in Kumarakom around 2008, linking resorts with local farmers, fishermen and craftspeople so that hotels bought vegetables, fish and services from the village. The state later expanded it into a Responsible Tourism Mission covering many destinations, with village experience packages where tourists watch coir-making, toddy tapping or paddy farming. The lesson for a small operator is simple: when local people earn from tourism, they welcome tourists, protect the destination and give visitors a richer, more real experience.

The Sectors of the Tourism Industry

The tourism industry is not one business but a group of connected sectors. If one link breaks, the whole trip suffers. A perfect hotel is useless if the train is cancelled.

  1. Transport: airlines (IndiGo, Air India), railways (Indian Railways, IRCTC), road transport (KSRTC, TGSRTC, private buses, taxis) and water transport (ferries, cruises, houseboats).
  2. Accommodation: hotels, resorts, homestays, hostels, guest houses and government tourism properties like Telangana Tourism's Haritha hotels.
  3. Food and beverage: restaurants, dhabas, cafes and catering.
  4. Attractions: monuments, national parks, theme parks, museums and events.
  5. Travel trade (intermediaries): tour operators, travel agents and online travel agencies (OTAs) such as MakeMyTrip, Yatra and ixigo.
  6. Support services: travel insurance, forex, guides, tourism boards, banks and telecom.

The tour operator sits in the middle of this web. Sushanth, for example, buys rooms from resorts, vehicles from transport owners and tickets from attractions, then puts them together into a package and sells it at one price. A travel agent, on the other hand, mainly sells other companies' products and earns a commission or service fee.

Types of Tourism

Tourism can be grouped by the main purpose of the trip. One trip can fit more than one type: a Kerala honeymoon is leisure tourism, but if the couple stays in a village and learns to cook appam, it also becomes rural and culinary tourism.

MICE stands for Meetings, Incentives, Conferences and Exhibitions. It is a high-value segment because companies pay for groups. Niche tourism refers to small, specialised markets such as birdwatching, film tourism, wildlife photography or food trails. Small operators often succeed by owning a niche rather than fighting big OTAs on general holidays.

Your business desk

Sushanth's packages already mix several types: Valparai is eco and nature tourism, Kerala is leisure and backwater tourism, and the Telangana heritage trip is cultural tourism built around places like Ramappa, Warangal Fort and Golconda. On the website, each package can carry a clear "type" tag such as "Nature escape" or "Heritage trail" so that a customer who loves temples does not scroll past the heritage tour while hunting for beaches. Over time, Sushanth can watch which tag brings the most WhatsApp enquiries and build more packages in that niche.

Global and Indian Tourism Organisations

Many organisations shape how tourism works. Knowing who they are helps an operator read the news, understand rules and spot opportunities.

Global Organisations

  • UN Tourism (formerly UNWTO, the World Tourism Organization): the United Nations agency for tourism, based in Madrid, Spain. It adopted the short name "UN Tourism" in 2024. It publishes the World Tourism Barometer, promotes sustainable tourism, and created the Global Code of Ethics for Tourism. World Tourism Day is celebrated on 27 September every year.
  • WTTC (World Travel & Tourism Council): a London-based forum of chief executives from big travel companies. It produces the yearly Economic Impact Research, with Oxford Economics, covering about 184 countries and economies.
  • IATA (International Air Transport Association): the trade association of the world's airlines, founded in 1945. It sets standards for tickets, airline codes and baggage, runs the settlement system through which travel agents pay airlines (BSP), and accredits travel agents to issue air tickets.
  • ICAO (International Civil Aviation Organization): a UN agency that sets global safety rules for civil aviation. Do not confuse it with IATA, which represents airlines as businesses.
  • PATA (Pacific Asia Travel Association): promotes tourism in the Asia-Pacific region.

Indian Organisations

  • Ministry of Tourism, Government of India: runs the Incredible India campaign, schemes like Swadesh Darshan, and a voluntary scheme for recognising tour operators, travel agents and hotels through the NIDHI+ portal.
  • ITDC (India Tourism Development Corporation): runs the Ashok Group of hotels and tourism services.
  • State tourism bodies: Telangana State Tourism Development Corporation (TGTDC), Kerala Tourism, Tamil Nadu Tourism and others run hotels, buses and packages.
  • Trade associations: TAAI (Travel Agents Association of India), IATO (Indian Association of Tour Operators), ADTOI (Association of Domestic Tour Operators of India) and FHRAI (for hotels and restaurants). These groups lobby the government and share updates on rules like GST.
Pro tip

Bookmark the Ministry of Tourism, UN Tourism and WTTC websites. When a customer or a bank officer asks "Is tourism really growing?", you can answer with official numbers instead of guesswork.

Words to Know

Tourism
Travel to places outside one's usual environment for less than a year, for leisure, business or other purposes.
Excursionist
A same-day visitor who does not stay overnight at the destination.
Domestic tourism
Residents travelling within their own country.
Inbound tourism
Foreign visitors travelling into a country.
Outbound tourism
Residents travelling to other countries.
Package tour
A trip that bundles transport, stay and often food and sightseeing for one price.
Multiplier effect
The way tourist spending passes through the local economy and creates further income.
MICE
Meetings, Incentives, Conferences and Exhibitions; business travel for groups.
Overtourism
When too many visitors harm a destination and the lives of local people.
UN Tourism
The United Nations agency for tourism, formerly called UNWTO.
